The AI landscapeWhat 'AI' means, how its layers nest, and the tools an AI application is built from.2
The nested AI landscape and the AI effectAI > machine learning > deep learning > generative AI > agentic AI; and 'AI is whatever hasn't been done yet', so name the task instead.

AI mimics intelligent behaviour, including hand-written rules. ML learns patterns from data. Deep learning uses layered neural networks that learn their own features from raw images or audio. GenAI creates content. Agentic AI loops: reason, act with tools, observe, repeat.

Tesler's 'AI effect': once a technique works reliably it gets a plain name (Deep Blue is now 'a chess engine'). So say what a system does (classifies, predicts, groups, generates, acts) and pick the layer by context.

Watch outCalling everything 'GenAI': it is expensive, and a simple supervised model often solves the problem better.

Types of machine learningSupervised, unsupervised, semi-supervised and reinforcement learning, chosen by what labels and feedback you have.4
Supervised learning: classification and regressionLearning from labelled examples to predict a category (classification) or a number (regression).

Like flashcards, each training row pairs features with a known answer, and the model learns a mapping for new rows. Classification outputs a class (fire / no fire); regression a continuous value (price, Fire Weather Index). The deciding question: do I have labelled historical examples of the outcome I want?

Semi-supervised learningA small labelled set guides learning over a large unlabelled pool, for when labels are expensive and raw data is cheap.

Labels often need expert time (radiologists, moderators) while raw data is abundant. The model anchors on a few labels and propagates them across unlabelled rows: 'label a sample, then propagate'. The course only asks you to recognise the situation it solves.

Reinforcement learningAn agent acts, receives rewards or penalties, and learns by trial and error a policy that maximises reward over a sequence of decisions.

How models learnFeatures and targets, the predict–measure–adjust loop, what is learned versus chosen, and how wrongness is measured.4
Features, target and labelsFeatures (X) are the evidence; the target (y) is what is predicted; labels are the known target values that teach the model.

Features are the columns fed in. Labels exist only for training and evaluation. Identifiers, the target and anything derived from it must stay out of X. One dataset can support several targets: salary for regression, or a high_salary flag (above the median) for classification.

The training loop and optimisationGuess, check, adjust, repeat: predict, measure the loss, nudge parameters to shrink it, until the loss is smallest.

Every model seeks the parameters with the smallest loss, by different routes. Linear regression solves directly; decision trees build rule by rule with greedy splits; logistic regression and neural networks step downhill with gradient descent, like walking blindfolded downhill by feeling the slope. The step size (learning rate) is a hyperparameter, and descent is why losses must be differentiable.

In the courseA person learns prices from 100 local sales; a model from 100,000 nationwide.
Source: Session 02, demo_02.ipynb, 01_ml_foundations_reference.ipynb
Parameters vs hyperparametersParameters (weights, split thresholds) are learned in training; hyperparameters (max_depth, number of trees) are set before. 'Tuning' means hyperparameters.

Hyperparameters are the exam conditions; parameters are the answers worked out during it. The algorithm changes parameters every round; you never set them by hand. You, or an automated search, set hyperparameters such as tree depth, number of trees, learning rate or number of clusters.

In the courseFWI = 0.8×Temperature − 0.3×Rain + 0.6×Wind + 3.2: four learned parameters.
Source: Session 02, demo_02.ipynb, challenge_02.ipynb
Loss functions vs evaluation metricsA loss is a differentiable number training minimises; a metric (accuracy, F1, R², RMSE) is a human-readable score computed afterwards.

MSE (regression) averages squared gaps, punishing big misses; it is outlier-sensitive, where MAE is robust. Cross-entropy (classification) costs −log(p) for the true class: about 0.01 at p = 0.99, 0.69 at 0.50 and 4.61 at 0.01, so confident wrong answers hurt most.

Watch outA classifier trained on cross-entropy never 'sees' its F1.
In the courseSquared gaps of 100, 25 and 900 give MSE ≈ 342; one house is 88% of the loss.

Statistics foundationsCentre, spread, correlation, probability and sample size: the statistics behind cleaning, scaling and judging results.4
Descriptive statistics and distributionsMean, median and mode describe the centre; variance, std and IQR the spread. Median and IQR resist outliers.

The mean is pulled by extremes; the median is the robust middle; the mode suits categories. IQR (Q3 − Q1) is the middle 50%. For a normal distribution the 68–95–99.7 rule gives the share within ±1, 2 and 3 std. Skewed variables (rain, salary) favour the median, MAE and robust scaling.

In the courseOne £850,000 CEO salary shifts the mean dramatically; the median barely moves.
Source: Session 02, 01_ml_foundations_reference.ipynb
Correlation, multicollinearity and causationCorrelation (−1 to +1) measures linear co-movement; correlated inputs distort linear coefficients; correlation never proves cause.

A heatmap during EDA suggests which features move with the target. Multicollinearity between inputs does not hurt trees but destabilises linear coefficients; Ridge, Lasso or dropping a feature helps. Look for confounders: ice-cream sales and drownings both rise in hot weather.

In the courseStyleForward's price–age correlation of −0.006 showed no relationship.
Source: Session 02, 01_ml_foundations_reference.ipynb; Session 03, demo_03_session.ipynb
Probability and conditional probabilityP(A | B) is the chance of A given B, which is exactly what a classifier estimates; a threshold turns it into a class.

A classifier outputs something like P(fire | these weather conditions), and the decision threshold converts that probability into a label. Grouping data by a condition and averaging a binary target gives an empirical P(target | condition), a quick EDA tool for spotting which variables carry signal.

In the courseP(returned | Fitted Dress) = 66% against about 33% for other products.
Source: Session 02, 01_ml_foundations_reference.ipynb
Sample size and the law of large numbersStatistics converge to the truth as samples grow; small samples give noisy metrics, so validate them with cross-validation.

Rules of thumb: at least 10× as many samples as features (per class), hundreds of rows for K-Means, 10,000+ for deep learning. On small data a few rows swing metrics, so use 5–10-fold cross-validation and read the spread; ±10% across folds means the headline is unreliable.

Watch outMore data narrows uncertainty but cannot add signal the features lack.

02Data Preparation & Feature EngineeringGetting data into a state a model can learn from honestly: quality checks, cleaning, encoding, scaling, and avoiding leakage.

This theme follows data from first inspection to a model-ready matrix: auditing quality, cleaning, encoding categories, scaling and engineering features. It then covers leakage, the silent failure that makes models look better than they are, and the pipelines that validate data before it reaches a model.

Data quality and explorationAuditing what you have before any model: quality, column roles, EDA and safe prototype data.4
Data quality and the missing-value auditAbout 80% of ML effort is data preparation; audit every column's missing rate against a threshold before modelling.

Missing values, duplicates, impossible values and bad labels degrade a model before any code is written. Ask: would an expert trust this data to decide from? A reusable report flags columns above a threshold (often 5%). Rule of thumb: under 5% impute; 5–20% impute with care; over 20% drop or add an is_missing flag. Clean is not correct: 0% missing can mean the source silently filled gaps.

Watch outDropping rows before asking why values are missing; the missing group can be meaningful.
In the courseproduct_price was 11.2% missing in the sample but 0% in the full file, a warning of silent filling.

Data cleaningFixing duplicates, formats, gaps, outliers and dates in a deliberate order.4
Cleaning order, duplicates and datesClean in a fixed order: duplicates, casing and formats, imputation, outliers, dates. Reordering introduces silent errors.

Duplicates bias statistics and leak into both train and test, so drop them first, before splitting, on a business key. Variant spellings ('YES', 'Yes') split one category into several, so standardise before imputing. Parse dates tolerantly (bad values become missing, not a crash), extract year or month, and drop the raw column. Work on a copy and print a before-and-after summary.

Watch outLeaving raw timestamps in the feature set.
In the course45 delivery records sent twice by the gateway were dropped before splitting.

Imputing missing valuesFill with the mean (symmetric data), median (skewed or outlier-prone) or mode (categories); investigate when values are not missing at random.

The median is the default for skewed variables such as salary. Above about 20% missing, imputation invents too much, so drop the column or add an is_missing flag. When missingness itself carries signal (people refusing to state a salary), blind imputation hides it and biases results.

In the courseMedian for experience_years and salary, mode for education_level.

Gaps in time series: an explicit ruleThere is no neutral way to fill missing sensor readings, only written-down choices, applied identically in training and serving.

Abstain for gaps longer than the signal's time scale. Forward-fill with a limit suits short gaps; a long limit hides real changes. Interpolate only offline, since it uses a future reading. Flag every fill, and build serving windows with the same helpers to avoid training-serving skew.

Watch outFilling silently, which 'quietly trains a model on invented data'.
In the courseAutoSort-AI fills at most one minute and abstains below 90% coverage of a 15-minute window.

Outliers: IQR detection, capping or removingFlag values below Q1 − 1.5×IQR or above Q3 + 1.5×IQR, then remove the row or cap (winsorise) it at the boundary.

IQR is robust to the extremes it detects. Remove clear entry errors when data is plentiful; cap to keep the row but limit its influence. Domain knowledge decides: a billionaire's salary is real, a negative one is not. Treat outliers before scaling, since MSE and means are dominated by them.

In the courseSalaries of −5,000 and 1,500,000 were capped at the IQR bounds.

Encoding categorical dataTurning categories into numbers without inventing order or exploding the column count.2
One-hot vs ordinal encodingOne-hot gives each nominal value its own 0/1 column; ordinal maps genuinely ordered categories to integers in their real order.

One-hot invents no order but widens the matrix; drop_first avoids the dummy variable trap (perfect multicollinearity) in linear models. Ordinal suits education level or staff grade, with the order stated explicitly and unknowns mapped to −1. Integer codes on nominal data (London=0, Paris=2) make models believe Paris > London.

Watch outOrdinal-encoding unordered categories, which invents a false ranking.
In the coursecompany_size one-hot encoded; staff_grade encoded AO → EO → HEO → SEO → Grade 7.
Source: Session 03, demo_03_session.ipynb, challenge_03_session.ipynb
High cardinality: choosing an encoding by level count2–10 levels: one-hot; 10–50: one-hot with rare levels pooled; 50+: out-of-fold target encoding. Ordinal only when order is real.

One-hot on hundreds of values creates sparse columns, raising cost and overfitting risk (the curse of dimensionality). Alternatives: group rare levels as 'Other', target encoding (fit on training data, cross-fitted), frequency or hash encoding, embeddings. Ignore unseen categories at prediction time, and compare encodings one change at a time.

In the courseaccount_id (499 levels) one-hot gave AUC 0.7062; out-of-fold target encoding gave 0.7266 in one column.

Scaling and feature engineeringPutting numbers on comparable scales, creating new signals, and wrapping prep so it repeats exactly.3
Feature scaling, and why scaling is not reshapingRescale numeric features so large ranges do not dominate distance- or coefficient-based models; trees need no scaling.

StandardScaler gives mean 0, std 1; MinMaxScaler maps to [0, 1] but is outlier-sensitive; RobustScaler uses median and IQR. Scale-sensitive: K-Means, KNN, SVM, linear and logistic regression, PCA, neural networks. These are linear transforms, so skew is unchanged; only a power transform (Yeo-Johnson) reshapes. Scale inputs, not the target.

Watch out'I scaled it so it would be normal' is false.
In the coursequeue_hours skew stayed +1.361 after both scalers and fell to −0.007 after Yeo-Johnson.

Feature engineeringCreate new columns (interactions, ratios, flags, bins, date parts) that encode domain knowledge the raw columns hide.

It often matters more than model choice. Add 1 to ratio denominators to avoid dividing by zero. Check each new feature against the target, and remember extra columns add noise. Engineered features obey leakage rules: no target or future information.

In the courseexp_skills_score = experience × skills topped the random forest's importance chart.

Drop identifiers (they carry no signal), raw datetimes and single-value columns. Leave one-hot columns unscaled: they are already 0/1. Split X and y with stratification so class balance matches. Returning the fitted scaler lets you transform new data identically, and encapsulation removes copy-paste errors across experiments.

Data leakageInformation from the test set or the future reaching training, so offline scores lie.4
Preprocessing and evaluation leakageFit every preprocessing step on training data only, tune on training data only, and evaluate on the test set once.

Common forms: fitting a scaler, encoder or PCA before splitting; cross-validating or tuning on data that includes the test set; repeatedly peeking at test scores. Leakage fails silently: no error, just over-optimistic numbers. Putting preprocessing inside a pipeline enforces the rule.

In the courseFitting on all SLA tickets inflated AUC by only +0.0005: 'believe the small one, and you will believe the big one'.
Source: Session 02, demo_02.ipynb; Session 09, demo_09_session.ipynb
Target leakage: only what exists at prediction timeA feature is legitimate only if its value exists when the prediction is made and is not a restatement of the target.

Some columns are consequences of the outcome or recorded afterwards; others are the target in another form. Ask of every column: 'would this exist at prediction time?' A leak can hide behind an innocent name, and it lifts test metrics while making the model useless in production.

Watch outCelebrating a suspiciously perfect score instead of treating it as a symptom.
In the courseAdding minutes_late (and post-delivery ratings) gave DeliveryWise a near-perfect, useless PR-AUC; fire-danger indices 'hand the classifier the answer'.
Source: Session 19, hackathon_19_session.ipynb; Session 02, demo_02.ipynb
Out-of-fold target encodingA rate computed from the target over all rows includes each row's own answer; compute target-derived features out-of-fold instead.

Replacing an ID with its historical outcome rate is sensible; the trap is including the row being predicted. Cross-validation then looks brilliant and collapses on new data, with no warning but a number that looks too good. Cross-fitting inside the pipeline means no row encodes itself.

In the courseNaive account breach-rate encoding gave CV AUC 0.8315 against an honest 0.6424: 'the lie was worth +0.1890'.

Point-in-time correct joinsEach training row may see only the newest value recorded at or before its prediction time, never later ones.

A feature store's key clause is 'timestamp ≤ as-of', taking the latest value per entity. For jittery feeds, align to a regular grid with a nearest match within a tolerance, label each interval by its end so joins look backwards, and compute rolling features from past steps only. 03Classical Machine Learning ModelsThe workhorse supervised and unsupervised algorithms: what each does, how it decides, and when to reach for it.

This theme covers the classical supervised models, from interpretable linear baselines to tree ensembles, then the clustering methods used to find segments. It ends with dimensionality reduction and anomaly detection, which compress data or flag the unusual.

Supervised modelsLinear baselines, decision trees and tree ensembles, and how to choose between them.5
Linear regression and reading coefficientsPredicts a number as a weighted sum of features plus an intercept, minimising MSE; on scaled features the coefficients show each feature's effect.

It is fast, stable on small data and a strong interpretable baseline: 'one std more temperature adds 2.2 FWI'. It assumes linear relationships (check residuals for curves) and roughly normal residuals. Correlated features split its coefficients, where Ridge and Lasso help. Coefficients that are tiny relative to the target's spread mean the features carry little signal.

In the courseFWI reached R² 0.593 against 0.699 for a depth-3 tree; StyleForward's largest price coefficient was worth 38p, meaning no signal.
Source: Session 02, demo_02.ipynb, challenge_02.ipynb
Logistic regressionModels the log-odds of a class as a weighted sum, passes it through a sigmoid to a probability, and thresholds it (default 0.5).

The standard interpretable baseline for binary classification: fast, with coefficients showing direction and strength, and probabilities alongside labels. It is trained on cross-entropy, needs scaled features and is regularised through C. Its linear decision boundary can underfit clearly non-linear patterns.

In the courseClassifying roles as above or below median salary, against a depth-5 tree.
Source: Session 03, demo_03_session.ipynb, challenge_03_session.ipynb
Decision trees and Gini impurityA sequence of yes/no questions, each split chosen to reduce impurity most; readable rules and no scaling needed.

At each node the tree tries every feature and threshold and picks the biggest gain in Gini impurity (1 − Σp², 0 when pure, 0.5 for a 50/50 binary node). It recurses until nodes are pure or a stopping rule (max_depth, min_samples_split) fires. Strengths: interpretable rules, scale invariance, feature importances.

Watch outTrees are fragile and overfit without depth limits: a few changed rows can reshape them.
In the courseFire rule: 'no rain and above roughly 31°C, predict fire'.
Source: Session 02, demo_02.ipynb, 03_decision_trees_explained.ipynb
Ensembles: random forests and gradient boostingBagging averages many decorrelated trees to cut variance; boosting adds small trees that each correct the last one's errors.

A random forest trains trees on bootstrap samples with random feature subsets and votes or averages; returns diminish above about 100 trees. Histogram gradient boosting is fast and strong on tabular data, tuned by learning_rate (correction size), max_leaf_nodes (complexity) and max_iter (number of trees). Tuning usually gains only a little over defaults.

Read regression errors in real units: RMSE and MAE are in target units; R² is variance explained.

Watch outExpecting tuning to transform a model.
In the courseForest CV F1 about 0.62 against 0.60 for one tree; a 12-run grid on California housing (MAE ≈ 0.32, about $32k).

Linear baseline vs non-linear modelCompare an interpretable linear model with a non-linear one, each in a pipeline that encodes and scales, and choose on more than the metric.

Wrapping each model in a pipeline (one-hot encoding plus scaling) means the preprocessing travels with it. Typical pairs are logistic regression against gradient boosting or a random forest. When scores are close, prefer the one that wins on simplicity, explainability, speed and maintenance.

Watch outShipping the complex model by reflex when the evidence shows no real gain.

ClusteringGrouping unlabelled records, choosing how many groups, and turning them into named, actionable segments.4
K-Means clusteringAssign each point to the nearest centroid, move centroids to their points' mean, repeat; minimises within-cluster sum of squares.

Fast and scalable to millions of rows, and each centroid reads as 'the typical member'. It is distance-based, so scaling is mandatory. It assumes roughly spherical, similar-sized clusters, must be told K, and forces every point, even outliers, into a cluster.

In the courseK=3 on scaled fire weather gave three nameable profiles (silhouette 0.304).
Source: Session 02, demo_02.ipynb
DBSCAN: density-based clusteringGroups densely packed points, finds the number of clusters itself, handles any shape, and labels isolated points as noise.

Set epsilon (neighbourhood radius) and min_samples (start near 2× features). Larger epsilon merges clusters; one big cluster means reduce it. Core points seed clusters and border points join them. Its noise points are a free anomaly list; scale first, and use HDBSCAN for varying densities.

Watch outTreating disagreement with K-Means as failure: 'a method that disagrees with your first choice is evidence'.
In the courseDBSCAN separated make_moons crescents where K-Means failed.
Source: Session 02, demo_02.ipynb
Choosing K: elbow and silhouetteShortlist K where inertia's fall flattens (the elbow), then pick with the silhouette score, which balances cohesion and separation.

Inertia always falls as K rises, so it cannot choose alone. Silhouette s = (b − a) / max(a, b) runs −1 to +1: above 0.5 strong, 0.25–0.5 reasonable, below 0.25 weak. If they disagree, prefer the K whose clusters you can name and act on.

In the courseStyleForward on age and price: K=3 topped silhouette (about 0.40), agreeing with the elbow.

Profiling clusters with a withheld labelDescribe each cluster by its feature means and name it; an outcome kept out of clustering can then interpret the segments.

Clustering finds structure; naming it is your job. The withheld label shows whether a segment carries risk without leaking into how clusters formed. There are no 'natural' clusters: the features chosen define what 'similar' means. Finish with an actionable, measurable recommendation.

Dimensionality reduction and anomaly detectionCompressing many features into a few, and flagging records that do not fit the bulk.2
PCA and its trade-offsCompresses scaled features into a few principal components that keep the most variance, saving time and noise at the cost of interpretability.

Each component blends all original features, so you can no longer say 'salary is driven by X'. Scale first and fit on training data only. On small, low-signal data dropping components can lose signal; on wide data it speeds training. t-SNE and UMAP are mainly for 2D visualisation.

Watch outUsing PCA when the goal is explanation.
In the courseSalary regression with 9 → 3 components slightly lowered accuracy.

Agent fundamentalsWhat an agent is, how its loop runs, and when it beats a chain.3

Metrics for generated textOverlap, meaning and grounding.2

Framing and scoping problemsDefine the problem and its value first; the technique follows.4
